## Problem

*Acquit* is a single-player card game based on the O.J. Simpson murder trial. The player is Simpson's defense attorney and tries to prove his innocence using evidence, witnesses, and credibility.

On a team of 6, I designed the gameplay systems: the scoring mechanics and the cards themselves. The challenge was making a courtroom defense feel like a strategic card game, where evidence, witnesses, and credibility interact in ways that are fair and interesting.

**Skills shown:** game design and game balancing.

## Constraint

- **Single player.** The game had to work with one person against the game itself.
- **Exactly 40 cards.** The whole game had to fit in a 40-card deck. This was the hardest of the three: every card had to earn its place, so each one had to carry both story and strategy.
- **It had to tell a story.** Playing it should walk the player through the case, not just test numbers.

## What I tested

I ran **five playtest sessions** to balance the game, adjusting the scoring and cards between sessions.

- **Finding: some cards were catch-alls.** A handful of evidence and witness cards worked against too many different arguments, so players could lean on them and skip the strategy.
- **Fix: reworked the arguments.** We changed the arguments themselves so that no small set of evidence or witness cards could serve as a universal solution. Players had to pick the right card for each argument.

## What failed

**I overscoped the design.** The original plan let the player take both sides of the case, prosecution and defense. Two complete sets of mechanics had to be fun on their own *and* balanced against each other, and I kept running into balance problems I couldn't solve in the time we had.

## What I changed

I cut the prosecution side and focused the game on one role: the defense.

- **One system to balance.** With one side, the mechanics could be designed and balanced as a single system.
- **Cards only had to work one way.** We'd struggled to come up with cards that made sense from both the prosecution's and the defense's perspective. Once the player was only the defense, every card only had to work from one side, which made it much easier to make all 40 cards count.

## What happened

The final game played smoothly, with enough variance in outcomes that a player who doesn't play their cards well will lose. The cases can't be won on autopilot.

For me, it was a chance to polish and practice my game design skills, especially designing within tight, specific constraints.

**What I'd do differently:** scope down early. Cutting the second side made the game better, not smaller.
