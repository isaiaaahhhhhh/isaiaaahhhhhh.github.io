**Skills shown:** game design and game balancing. I designed the game's systems, including the
scoring mechanics and the cards, then balanced them over five playtest sessions.

## Problem

*Acquit* is a single-player card game based on the O.J. Simpson murder trial. The player is
Simpson's defense attorney and tries to prove his innocence using **evidence**, **witnesses**, and
**credibility**.

On a team of 6, I designed the gameplay systems: the **scoring mechanics** and the **cards**
themselves. The challenge was making a courtroom defense feel like a strategic card game, where
evidence, witnesses, and credibility interact in ways that are fair and interesting.

## Constraint

- **Single player.** The game had to work with one person against the game itself.
- **Exactly 40 cards.** The whole game had to fit in a 40-card deck.
- **It had to tell a story.** Playing it should walk the player through the case, not just
  test numbers.

**The 40-card limit was the hardest of the three.** Every card had to earn its place, so each one
had to carry both story and strategy.

## What I tested

I ran **five playtest sessions** to balance the game, adjusting the scoring and cards between sessions.
The biggest finding: **some cards could be used against too many different arguments.** A handful
of evidence and witness cards worked almost everywhere, so players could lean on them as a
catch-all answer and skip the strategy. We reworked the **arguments** themselves so that no small
set of evidence or witness cards could serve as a universal solution. Players had to pick the right
card for each argument.

## What failed

**I overscoped the design.** The original plan let the player take **both** sides of the case,
prosecution and defense. Two complete sets of mechanics had to be fun on their own *and* balanced
against each other, and I kept running into balance problems I couldn't solve in the time we had.

## What I changed

I cut the prosecution side and focused the game on **one role: the defense**. With one side, the
mechanics could be designed and balanced as a single system.

It also solved a card-design problem. We'd struggled to come up with cards that made sense from both
the prosecution's **and** the defense's perspective. Once the player was only the defense, every
card only had to work from one side. That made it much easier to make all 40 cards count.

## What happened

The final game **played smoothly**, with **enough variance in outcomes that a player who doesn't play
their cards well will lose**. The cases can't be won on autopilot.

For me, it was a chance to polish and practice my game design skills, especially **designing within
tight, specific constraints**. The lesson I'm keeping is to scope down early. Cutting the second
side made the game better, not smaller.
